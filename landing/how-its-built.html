How it's built

The landing page says what Roadbook does. This page says how the drawing is made, for anyone who wants to check the claim before trusting a line on a map.

Every line says how it is known

A trip on a Roadbook map is not one line. It is a list of stretches, and each stretch carries one of four kinds. The kind survives from the moment the record is read all the way to the pixels, and it is drawn in a different ink and a different stroke, so the two can never be confused.

  • Observed

    The phone recorded positions along this stretch. The line joins them exactly as recorded; nothing is smoothed, nothing is added.

  • Routed

    Nothing was recorded between two positions, so the line follows the roads between them. It is drawn over a paper casing so it cannot be mistaken for a recording, and its distance is the road's, not a measurement.

  • Unknown

    Nothing was recorded and no road was looked up. A dashed straight line marks the gap, and says exactly that instead of guessing.

  • Air

    A flight, told by its speed. The dotted arc is the shortest path between where it began and ended, never pretending to be a road.

The rule behind the four: a gap drawn as a confident solid line would be a lie. Most tools in this category draw one anyway. Roadbook is built so it cannot — the kind is part of the data, not a styling choice, and every legend on every map repeats these four words.

Where the figures come from

Under every distance on a trip's page sits a thin bar in the same four inks, showing how much of that distance the phone recorded and how much was drawn in. The total never appears without it. Every number on a page — kilometres, days, stops, time away — is printed by the same command-line tool from the same stored record, so any figure can be reproduced outside the browser. The map on the landing page and the three trips under it are a fictional record committed to the repository; every number in them reproduces from one command.

Where your data lives

Hosted Roadbook is one service with an account per person. You sign in with Google; Roadbook never sees a password. Every row of your data carries your account, every read and write in the service is filtered by it, and a standing family of tests proves that one account cannot see or touch another's. The only way anything of yours becomes visible is a share link you create, and you can revoke it.

Your export streams to the server and nowhere else, and is retained because it may be the only copy of your history that still exists. Photos are never kept — only their positions, times and small thumbnails. Leaving is one control on your own page: your account and every row and file that was yours are removed. Nothing lingers in a provider's analytics, because there are none: the landing page and this one carry no scripts, no cookies, no trackers.

There is no public try-it demo, on purpose. An open upload endpoint for strangers' location histories would be a privacy problem dressed as a convenience. The screenshot on the landing page and the one-command demo in the repository are the substitute.

Run it yourself

Roadbook is open source under AGPL-3.0, and self-hosting is the reference deployment — the hosted service runs the same docker compose up. The repository's README starts you with the demo dataset.
